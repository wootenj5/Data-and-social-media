Misinformation and Public Opinion on Social Media

Source 1

URLSocial Media and News Fact Sheet
Site AuthorPew Research Center
AbstractThis Pew Research Center fact sheet presents survey data about how Americans use social-media platforms for news. It reports that many adults say they get news from social media at least sometimes and compares which platforms people use most often for news. The page also includes information about differences by age, political affiliation, and other demographics. This source is useful because it gives evidence that social media is an important part of the modern news environment. When people encounter news through a feed, the design of that platform can affect what information receives attention. The data does not prove that social media determines people's beliefs, but it explains why misinformation and content-ranking decisions can influence public discussion.

Source 2

URLScaling Up Fact-Checking Using the Wisdom of Crowds
Site AuthorGordon Pennycook and David G. Rand
AbstractThis research article studies whether groups of ordinary people can help identify misleading online news stories. The authors compare crowd judgments with the assessments of professional fact-checkers and examine whether this approach could help evaluate a larger number of stories. The study is useful because fact-checking every social-media post is difficult for any one organization. It suggests that carefully designed crowd ratings may assist professional fact-checking instead of replacing it. This source supports the idea that reducing misinformation needs more than a single warning label or moderator. Platforms can combine research, expert review, user feedback, and clear explanations to make information environments more trustworthy while still allowing people to question and discuss important public issues.
